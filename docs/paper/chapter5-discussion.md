# Chapter 5: Discussion

## 5.1 Introduction

Chapter 4 established what happened when two autonomous coding agents evolved a full-stack codebase under controlled conditions: immediate and mechanism-specific constraint violations, a task-driven rather than uniformly accumulating erosion trajectory, no statistically defensible effect of prompt-embedded architectural guidance, sharply different violation profiles per agent, an empty silent-decay class, and a gap between self-reported completion and verified delivery. This chapter interprets those findings against the literature (§5.2), states the limits within which the interpretations hold (§5.3), and sets out the research programme those limits imply (§5.4). The evidence itself is not restated here; every claim below traces to a section of Chapter 4.

## 5.2 Interpreting the Principal Findings

### 5.2.1 Architectural erosion under agents is immediate and mechanism-specific

The erosion literature describes architectural decay in human-maintained systems as gradual: a slow divergence between intended and implemented architecture accumulated over many maintenance cycles (Perry and Wolf, 1992; Eick et al., 2001; de Silva and Balasubramaniam, 2012; Li et al., 2022). The behaviour observed in this study does not match that temporal profile. Every trajectory violated the rulepack at its first task, and the dominant violations were not diffuse quality loss but three specific, repeatable engineering omissions: raising service failures outside the unified exception hierarchy, modifying persistent entities without generating the matching migration, and introducing near-duplicate implementations of existing logic (§4.2).

This pattern aligns more closely with the emerging empirical picture of AI-assisted development than with classical drift. Studies of agent-generated code report elevated static-analysis findings and complexity at the moment of introduction (Agarwal, He and Vasilescu, 2026), recurring structural smells in LLM output (Zhu, Tsantalis and Rigby, 2026), and early-appearing technical-debt signals rather than late-life decay (Ehsani et al., 2026); industry telemetry likewise records rising duplication in AI-assisted codebases (GitClear, 2026). The contribution of the present result is specificity: under a rule-linked instrument, "agents erode architecture" resolves into a short list of named rule failures with identifiable engineering countermeasures, rather than a general quality decline. Erosion in this setting is therefore better understood as *systematic omission of cross-cutting obligations* — the conventions that connect a change to the rest of the system, such as migrations and shared exception types — than as the entropy of long maintenance histories. That distinction matters because omissions of this kind are checkable at commit time, which is precisely what the following subsection argues for.

### 5.2.2 Prompt-embedded rules did not secure conformance

The Structured condition placed the architectural rules and the API contract directly in the task prompt, and its effect was directional at best: a median reduction of 1.5 violations per task, no statistical support at this sample size, and constraint failure in every run regardless (§4.3). Whatever benefit explicit guidance conferred, it did not change the categorical outcome even once.

This bears directly on the question that motivated the study's design: whether architectural constraints belong in the prompt or in an external enforcement mechanism. The answer suggested here is that prompt-level declaration is not a substitute for mechanical verification. This is consistent with the broader observation that current agent evaluation and steering practices centre on functional signals while structural properties go unassessed (Jiang et al., 2026; Le et al., 2026), and it supports the case for continuous, automated conformance checking of the kind proposed for architecture generally (Bucaioni et al., 2024) — applied, in this setting, as a gate on agent output. For practitioners the implication is concrete: teams delegating iterative work to coding agents should treat architectural rules the way they treat failing tests — as machine-checked gates in the delivery pipeline — rather than relying on instruction-following, however detailed the instructions. The self-correction observed at T3 (§4.5) suggests agents are capable of repairing violations when the task brings them into view; an enforcement loop that surfaces violations explicitly would convert that latent capability into a systematic behaviour, a design taken up in §5.4.

### 5.2.3 Agent-specific architectural personalities

Ranked by volume alone, Claude outperformed Codex by a factor of two to four (§4.4). The more useful finding is qualitative: the two agents fail differently. Codex's violations are dominated by a single-category monoculture — every service-layer failure raised outside the project's exception hierarchy, 62 findings from one rule across six evaluations, under both prompt strategies — while Claude never triggered that rule at all, and instead contributed the dataset's only layering and complexity drift. Duplication was the one weakness common to both, consistent with clone-heavy generation being a general property of current code-generating models (Zhu, Tsantalis and Rigby, 2026; GitClear, 2026) and with the established evidence that clones are defect-prone rather than merely inelegant (Juergens et al., 2009).

Two consequences follow. For practice, review effort should be profiled to the agent in use: a team running Codex-like agents on a NestJS codebase should audit exception paths and migrations first; a team running Claude-like agents should watch dependency direction and unit complexity. Generic "AI code review" checklists discard exactly the information this experiment shows to be most discriminating. For research, agent identity is a first-class experimental variable, not a nuisance factor: aggregating violations across agents (as benchmark-style leaderboards implicitly do) would have averaged the ERR monoculture into invisibility.

### 5.2.4 Degradation follows task structure, with partial self-correction

No trajectory showed the accelerating, convex decay that Lehman's (1980) increasing-complexity law predicts for evolving systems in the absence of countervailing effort. Instead, cumulative violations spiked at T2 — the relational remodel whose design deliberately stressed contract stability and duplication — and partially receded at T3, where three of four conditions resolved more violations than they introduced (§4.5). Within this horizon, erosion is demand-driven: its rate reflects what the current task structurally requires, not how degraded the codebase already is.

Two readings are compatible with this result, and the data cannot separate them. The optimistic reading is that agents, unlike the schedule-pressured maintainers of classical erosion accounts (van Gurp and Bosch, 2002), re-derive their picture of the codebase at every task and therefore do not compound confusion the way human teams do. The cautious reading is that three tasks are simply too short for feedback effects to surface: Lehman's law operates over release histories, not over three iterations. The unprompted repair at T3 is small but consistent in direction, and is arguably the most encouraging single observation in the dataset — it indicates that the capability needed for an enforcement loop (§5.2.2) already exists in the agents themselves.

### 5.2.5 The silent-decay null result

The study's central methodological hypothesis — that binary constraints pass while continuous metrics decay — was not observed, because its precondition never occurred: no run passed the constraint gate (§4.6). This null does not refute the two-layer measurement design, but it does sharpen what the design is for. Against tasks deliberately built to stress the rulepack and a strict constraint set, binary rules saturated first; the metric layer's contribution in this sample was severity grading and corroboration, not independent detection. Both live explanations should be carried forward honestly: constraint saturation may be an artefact of stress-designed tasks (in which case silent decay remains testable on gentler, more realistic task mixes), or binary constraints may genuinely dominate first-line erosion detection (in which case the metric layer's role is longitudinal grading rather than discovery). Distinguishing the two requires runs that pass constraints — which, at this rulepack strictness, means either cleaner agents or less adversarial tasks.

### 5.2.6 Completion claims versus verified outcomes

All twelve runs declared success; of the four runs with an independent acceptance suite, three failed it (§4.8). Self-reported completion is therefore not a delivery signal, echoing the concern that agent-facing evaluation must be independent of the agent's own account of its work (Li, Zhang and Hassan, 2025; Zeng et al., 2025). Combined with the efficiency data — Codex roughly twice as fast and two to four times as violation-prone — the results reproduce, in miniature, the volume–quality tension reported for agent-driven development at scale (Zhu, Tsantalis and Rigby, 2026). The comparison is asymmetric (token and cost data are unavailable for one CLI) and rests on a single verifiable task, so it is stated as a caution rather than a conclusion: speed and self-confidence are the two signals most visible to an adopting team, and both were misleading here.

## 5.3 Limitations

The following limitations bound every claim above. They are grouped by validity type, continuing §3.6.

**Construct validity.** Architectural integrity is operationalised as conformance to one rulepack: nineteen concerns encoding one defensible interpretation of layered full-stack architecture for one business domain. The rules are entangled with the business the codebase serves and with human architectural judgment; no rule set of any size exhausts "architectural integrity", and a different but equally defensible rulepack could shift both the violation counts and their category profile. Two retained metrics are proxies rather than direct measurements (the mock-per-test-case ratio for test discipline; the propagation-incompleteness ratio for cross-stack change), and the cross-stack rulepack ships at experimental maturity. The cross-stack layer's zero findings (§4.2) must therefore be read with particular care: the data cannot distinguish genuine contract discipline from instrument insensitivity.

**Internal validity.** The Structured prompt varies two blocks jointly — the API contract and the architectural guidance — by design (§3.5); without an ablation, observed differences cannot be attributed to either block individually, nor can it be determined which specific rules, if any, altered agent behaviour. The baseline carries five pre-existing frontend findings; net-delta accounting removes them from the counts, but cannot rule out that agents *imitated* inherited violation patterns (notably the pre-existing clones) rather than generating them independently, which complicates attribution of the DUP category. Finally, the two agent CLIs are not parameter-identical: reasoning effort was pinned to high on both and personalisation disabled, but the CLIs differ in tooling surface and observability (turn and cost data exist for one only), so the agent comparison includes an irreducible platform component.

**External validity.** The study examined one codebase, in one architectural style (a layered TypeScript/JavaScript monolith), in one domain (CRM), with two agents at fixed model snapshots, over three tasks. Nothing here licenses claims about microservice architectures, other languages, other domains, later model versions, or longer horizons. The study is designed as a reusable paradigm demonstrated on an exemplar, not as a general benchmark, and its findings should be generalised only by re-running the paradigm elsewhere.

**Conclusion validity.** Each condition was executed once: there is no replication, every trajectory-level contrast rests on n = 1 per cell, and the Wilcoxon comparisons cannot reach significance at three pairs per agent by construction (§4.3). Trajectories are three points long, which forbids formal curve analysis (§4.5). Independent functional verification exists for T1 only, and the three tasks differ in size (approximately 24, 40, and 39 requirement items respectively), so cross-task comparisons of raw counts partially reflect task magnitude. The Judgments layer — the instrument that would grade functional compliance — was designed but not implemented, so the architecture–correctness relationship could not be analysed. An agent self-review probe (T5) was designed and piloted, but its data were not retained in the final dataset and it contributes nothing to the reported results.

## 5.4 Future Work

Each item below is traceable to a limitation above, ordered by what unlocks what.

1. **Replication across sessions.** Repeating each condition across independent sessions is the single highest-value extension: it converts every directional observation in §4.3–§4.5 into a testable one and is a precondition for the interaction analysis (§4.4) and for stabilising the metric-correlation structure (§4.7).
2. **Implementing the Judgments layer.** With graded functional compliance available, the architecture × correctness quadrant analysis already specified in the analysis plan becomes possible — in particular the "functionally correct but architecturally degraded" quadrant that speaks directly to the volume–quality tension (Zhu, Tsantalis and Rigby, 2026).
3. **Prompt-block ablation.** Separating the API-contract block from the architectural-rules block, and subsetting the rules themselves, would identify which guidance (if any) changes agent behaviour — resolving the joint-treatment ambiguity acknowledged in §5.3.
4. **Longer task chains.** Testing Lehman-style acceleration requires horizons long enough for feedback effects: composite multi-ticket tasks and chains of five or more iterations on the same workspace.
5. **Enforcement-in-the-loop.** The most direct design consequence of §5.2.2 and the T3 self-correction result: feed harness findings back to the agent after each task and measure whether violations are repaired and at what cost. This converts the harness from a measurement instrument into an intervention, and tests the "external gate" recommendation experimentally.
6. **Instrument extension.** Additional rulepacks (microservices, other languages) and agents to probe external validity; maturation of the cross-stack rulepack beyond experimental status, including revisiting the four pruned cross-stack categories, so that the zero-findings result in §4.2 becomes interpretable.
7. **Self-assessment calibration.** Reinstating the T5 self-review probe with retained data would quantify whether agents can be trusted to audit their own architectural output — a question §4.8's completion-verification gap makes pointed.

## 5.5 Summary

The findings admit a compact interpretation. Architectural erosion under autonomous agents is real but not mysterious: it is immediate, concentrated in a few named cross-cutting obligations, shaped by the structural demands of the task at hand, and partially self-correcting when the task surfaces the damage. Prompt-level rule declaration did not prevent it; the two agents committed it in distinctly different ways; and the agents' own completion claims were unreliable guides to what was delivered. Within the stated limits — one codebase, one run per condition, three tasks — the practical consequence is consistent across every finding: teams should pair coding agents with external, rule-linked conformance gates and agent-profiled review, and the research community should treat structural conformance as a first-class, independently measured dimension of agent evaluation. Chapter 6 concludes.
